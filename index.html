01

A maze is *a graph*

Every open cell is a dot. Every open passage between two cells is a line. Solving the maze means finding a path through that graph, so everything we know about graphs applies.

Check yourself

What do the dots and lines stand for? Dots are open cells and lines are open passages between them.

02

Stack *or* queue?

Both hold the cells a search has found but not yet explored. A stack gives back the newest item. A queue gives back the oldest. That single choice is the whole difference between DFS and BFS.

Check yourself

Which one finds the shortest path? The queue (BFS), because it spreads out evenly from the start.

03

How a maze *grows*

A walker moves to a random unvisited room and knocks down the wall between. When it is stuck, it steps back along its stack. Every room is joined exactly once, so there is exactly one route between any two rooms.

Check yourself

Why is there only one route? Each room joins the maze exactly once, so no loops can form.

04

BFS *spreads*, DFS *dives*

BFS uses a queue: it explores in rings and always finds the shortest path. DFS uses a stack: it follows one corridor to the end before trying another. With one route through the maze both find it, but DFS can wander far more.

Check yourself

Why might DFS explore more cells? It commits to a corridor and may follow a long wrong branch before backing up.

05

A* makes *a guess*

Dijkstra explores by distance travelled. A* adds a guess of the distance left: the straight-line grid distance to the goal. Drag the loops slider. With more ways through, the guess gets sharper and A* skips more of the maze.

Check yourself

When does A* help least? When the real route winds a lot, because the straight-line guess is far too optimistic.
